Used to · Would · Be used to · Get used to

Four look-alike structures, four different jobs. Here's how to tell them apart — and use them correctly.

1️⃣ USED TO + V1

A past habit or state that is NOT true now.

Subject + used to + V1
Examples
  • I used to live in London. (Now I don't.)
  • She used to play tennis every weekend.
  • There used to be a cinema here.
✗ I use to like coffee.
✓ I didn't use to like coffee. / Did you use to play football?
💡 Used to = past only.
2️⃣ WOULD + V1 (Past Habit)

Repeated actions in the past.

Subject + would + V1
Examples
  • Every summer we would visit our grandparents.
  • My father would tell us funny stories.
  • When I was a child, I would play outside for hours.
⚠️ Would CANNOT describe past states.
✗ I would have long hair.
✓ I used to have long hair.
✗ There would be a park here.
✓ There used to be a park here.
3️⃣ BE USED TO + Noun / V-ing

Be accustomed to; something feels normal.

Subject + be + used to + noun / V-ing
Examples
  • I'm used to cold weather.
  • She's used to working late.
  • We aren't used to spicy food.
💡 After be used to, use a noun or verb + ing.
✗ I'm used to work late.
✓ I'm used to working late.
4️⃣ GET USED TO + Noun / V-ing

Become accustomed to something — the process of adapting.

Subject + get + used to + noun / V-ing
Examples
  • I'm getting used to my new job.
  • You'll get used to waking up early.
  • It took me two months to get used to driving on the left.
💡 "Get used to" = adaptation is happening.
🧠 Memory Tip
FunctionModal
used to + V1 = Past (finished)be used to + V-ing = It's normal now
would + V1 = Repeated past actions only (NOT states)get used to + V-ing = Becoming normal

Present Modal Verbs

Obligation, advice, permission, prohibition and possibility — the everyday modal verbs, side by side.

1️⃣ Obligation & Necessity
MUST

Strong obligation (often the speaker's opinion).

must + V1
  • You must wear a seatbelt.
  • I must finish this report today.
HAVE TO

External obligation (rules, laws, situations).

have to + V1
  • Students have to wear uniforms.
  • I have to get up early for work.
⚖️ Difference

must → the speaker decides  |  have to → a rule or situation decides

  • I must call my parents. (my decision)
  • I have to wear a uniform. (school rule)
2️⃣ Lack of Obligation
DON'T HAVE TO

It is not necessary.

don't / doesn't have to + V1
  • You don't have to come tomorrow.
  • She doesn't have to cook today.
NEEDN'T

Also means not necessary.

needn't + V1
  • You needn't hurry.
  • We needn't buy any bread.
💡 don't have to = needn't
3️⃣ Advice & Recommendation
SHOULD

Used for advice.

should + V1
  • You should study more.
  • We should leave now.
SHOULDN'T

Negative advice.

  • You shouldn't eat too much sugar.
  • He shouldn't drive so fast.
OUGHT TO

Similar to should, but slightly more formal.

ought to + V1
  • You ought to apologize.
  • We ought to help them.
⚠️ Usually not used in negatives or questions.
4️⃣ Permission
CAN

Permission.

  • You can use my laptop.
  • Students can leave now.
MAY

Formal permission.

  • You may leave now.
  • May I come in?
5️⃣ Prohibition
CAN'T

Not allowed.

  • You can't park here.
  • Visitors can't enter this area.
6️⃣ Possibility & Probability
MAY

50% possibility.

  • It may rain later.
MIGHT

Less certain possibility.

  • She might come tomorrow.
COULD

Possible.

  • This could be the answer.
  • They could arrive late.

Past Modals of Deduction

We can use may / must / can't / might / could + have + past participle (V3) to make deductions about something in the past.

🔎 Three Key Sentences
SentenceWhat it shows
1 It's awfully quiet up there. I think the children must have fallen asleep.must have + V3 → I feel certain it happened
2 Do you think he could have heard us? He doesn't look very pleased.could have + V3 → it possibly happened
3 Go back and look for it. You might have dropped it in the car.might have + V3 → it possibly happened
1. MUST HAVE + V3 — I'm certain it happened
must have + past participle

We use must have + V3 when we feel we are certain something happened or is true.

Subject + must have + V3
Examples
  • James isn't in his office. He must have just left.
  • I didn't know Olivia lived in Berlin. She must've moved there quite recently.
🗣 Speaking & informal writing

We generally use 've instead of the full form have.

  • He must've been mistaken.
  • They might've got lost.
2. CAN'T / COULDN'T HAVE + V3 — I'm certain it did NOT happen
can't have / couldn't have + past participle

We use can't / couldn't have + V3 when we feel we are certain something didn't happen or wasn't true.

Subject + can't / couldn't have + V3
Examples
  • Jim wasn't at the meeting this morning, which isn't like him. He can't have known about it.
  • You couldn't have seen Alice earlier. She's in Dubai at the moment.
must not have

We can sometimes use must not have in the same way. However, we do not usually use the contracted form mustn't have.

  • They must not have read the notice.
✗ They mustn't have read the notice.
✓ They must not have read the notice.
3. MIGHT / COULD / MAY HAVE + V3 — it possibly happened
might have / could have / may have + past participle

We use might / could / may have + V3 when we think something possibly happened or was possibly true.

Subject + might / could / may have + V3
Examples
  • She's late. She might've missed the bus.
  • I've looked everywhere for my phone. I suppose I could've left it in the taxi.
  • They don't know what to do. They may not have understood the instructions correctly.
⚠️ We do NOT use can have for possibility in the past.
✗ She can have missed the bus.
✓ She might / could / may have missed the bus.
📋 Quick Summary
MeaningModal + have + V3
Certain it happened / is truemust have + V3
Certain it did NOT happen / wasn't truecan't have / couldn't have + V3 (also: must not have)
Possibly happened / possibly truemight / could / may have + V3
Possibly did NOT happenmight not / may not have + V3

TOPIC 4 OF 9

Wish & If Only

We use wish and if only to express regrets, unreal situations, or a desire for things to be different. If only carries the same meaning as wish, but expresses a stronger emotion.

WISH + Past Simple (Present Regrets)

Talk about situations we want to be different now.

wish + Past Simple
Examples
  • I wish I lived nearer my university.
  • She wishes she had more free time.
  • I wish I could play the guitar.
  • I wish it wasn't raining.
💡 Meaning: the present situation is different from what I want.
WISH + Past Perfect (Past Regrets)

Express regret about something that happened (or didn't happen) in the past.

wish + had + V3
Examples
  • I wish I had studied harder.
  • She wishes she hadn't sold her car.
  • We wish we had left earlier.
💡 Meaning: you cannot change the past.
WISH + WOULD (Complaints & Desired Changes)

Talk about things we want someone or something else to change.

wish + subject + would + V1
Examples
  • I wish you would listen to me.
  • She wishes her neighbours would stop making noise.
  • We wish the bus would arrive on time.
💡 Often expresses annoyance, dissatisfaction, or hope for change.
⚠️ Would is NOT used when the subject and object are the same person.
✗ I wish I would speak French.
✓ I wish I could speak French.
IF ONLY

Follows the same grammar rules as wish, but sounds more emotional or emphatic.

Present:

  • If only I had more money!
  • If only she were here!

Past:

  • If only we had booked earlier.
  • If only I hadn't forgotten his birthday.

Complaints:

  • If only they would stop arguing!

Third & Mixed Conditionals

We use the Third Conditional to talk about imaginary or unreal situations in the past. It describes how the past could have been different — but the situation is impossible to change, because it is already in the past.

Third Conditional (Past → Past)

We use the Third Conditional to imagine a different past and its different past result.

If + Past Perfect, would have + V3  /  would have + V3 if + Past Perfect
  • If I had studied harder, I would have passed the exam.
  • If they had left earlier, they wouldn't have missed the train.
  • She would have come if you had invited her.
💡 Meaning: neither action happened.
Mixed Conditional (Past → Present)

Used when a past action has a present result.

If + Past Perfect, would + V1
  • If I'd had some breakfast, I wouldn't be so hungry now.
  • If she had accepted the job, she would work in London now.
  • If we'd caught the earlier train, we would be home now.
💡 Meaning: the past cannot change, but it affects the present.
Mixed Conditional (Present → Past)

Used when a present unreal situation affected the past.

If + Past Simple, would have + V3
  • If I had more money, I would have bought that car.
  • If he were more organized, he wouldn't have forgotten the meeting.
  • If we lived nearer, we would have visited you yesterday.
💡 Meaning: the present situation is different, so the past result was different.

Adjectives & Adverbs

Adjectives describe people and things. Adverbs tell us more about verbs — and also about adjectives, other adverbs, or a whole sentence.

🔎 Four Key Sentences
SentenceWhat it shows
1 He's a remarkably successful writer. He's sold millions of books.remarkably = adverb of degree, modifying the adjective successful
2 Emer frequently pops in for a chat. I think she must be a bit lonely.frequently = adverb of frequency (before the main verb)
3 I'm meeting Kurt at the theatre at 7.00 p.m. tomorrow evening.place (at the theatre) comes before time (7.00 p.m. tomorrow evening)
4 Don't push so hard! You'll break it.hard = an adverb with the same form as the adjective
Adjectives

We use adjectives to add descriptive detail to people or things. They can be used in three positions:

1. Before a noun
adjective + noun
Examples
  • We had a fantastic time.
  • She bought a beautiful dress.
2. After a noun / pronoun in questions with to be
Was / Is + noun + adjective?
Examples
  • Was the hotel expensive?
  • Were the tickets cheap?
3. After a linking verb

be, seem, look, feel, sound, become, get

Examples
  • Did the new guy seem nice?
  • Hurry up – it's getting late!
  • You look tired.
⚠ Not all adjectives fit all positions

Some adjectives are used in only one position.

✗ The reason is main.
✓ It's the main reason. (before a noun only)
✗ an asleep child
✓ The child is asleep. (after a verb only)
After linking verbs use an adjective

Not an adverb — the adjective describes the subject.

✗ She looks beautifully.
✓ She looks beautiful.
✗ It sounds strangely.
✓ It sounds strange.
Adverbs — what do they tell us?

We use adverbs in a number of ways. Here are the main types:

Manner — HOW?

How something happens or is done.

Most commonly after the verb and its object. Adverbs ending in -ly can sometimes go before the verb.

Examples
  • The roads were icy, so we drove very carefully.
  • He was driving at over 150 kmh.
  • She carefully opened the box. (before the verb)
Frequency — HOW OFTEN?

How often something is done.

Generally before the main verb, but after to be.

always · usually · often · sometimes · rarely · never

Examples
  • It sometimes rains here.
  • The meetings are usually in Room 10.
  • I rarely go out at night.
Time — WHEN?

When something happens.

now · today · yesterday · soon · just · already · yet

Examples
  • I've just emailed you those photos.
  • She arrived yesterday.
Place — WHERE?

Where something happens.

here · there · outside · abroad · nearby · everywhere

Examples
  • I saw Jenny in town yesterday.
  • Please wait outside.
Time AND Place together

When you mention both, the position can depend on the verb. For verbs with a strong connection with place (go, come, arrive, leave, stay, live) we generally put the place before the time.

verb + PLACE + TIME
Examples
  • We went to London at the weekend.
  • She arrived at the airport yesterday.
  • They moved to Canada in 2020.
💡 With other verbs the order is more flexible: I saw Jenny in town yesterday.
Degree — HOW MUCH?

Shows the degree or extent of something. Used with adjectives, adverbs and verbs.

It goes directly before the word it modifies.

very · really · quite · absolutely · extremely · completely

Examples
  • It's really sunny today.
  • I'm absolutely freezing!
  • He's a remarkably successful writer.
Attitude — WHAT'S MY OPINION?

Some adverbs express the speaker's attitude towards something.

actually · fortunately · honestly · obviously · personally

Examples
  • I disagree with you, actually.
  • Fortunately, nobody was hurt.
  • Personally, I prefer tea.
📋 Adverb Quick Reference
TypeAnswers…Usual positionExample
MannerHow?after the verb (+ object); -ly adverbs may go before the verbdrove very carefully

⚠ Common Confusions
Adverbs WITHOUT -ly

Adverbs often end in -ly, but not always:

often · never · yesterday · now · everywhere · nearby

Adjectives ENDING in -ly

friendly · likely · lonely · lovely · silly · ugly (also lively, elderly)

⚠️ These are adjectives, not adverbs.
✗ She spoke friendly to me.
✓ She spoke to me in a friendly way.
SAME form: adjective & adverb

daily · early · hard · fast · weekly

  • I get a daily paper. (adjective)
  • I go there daily. (adverb)
💡 Irregular: good (adj.) → well (adv.): She sings well.
hard ≠ hardly  |  late ≠ lately

Adding -ly can change the meaning completely:

  • Don't push so hard! (= with a lot of force)  vs  I hardly know him. (= almost not at all)
  • We arrived late. (= after the expected time)  vs  I haven't seen her lately. (= recently)

Compound Adjectives

A compound adjective is made of two words that work together as one adjective. They are usually written with a hyphen, but occasionally as a single word.

What do they look like?
Examples
  • a well-known band
  • a fixed-wheel bicycle
  • a handmade gift (one word)
  • a brand new phone (two words, no hyphen)
💡 Think of them as a short description: a bicycle with a fixed wheel → a fixed-wheel bicycle.
5 Common Forms
1. … + past participle
word + V3
Examples
  • high-heeled shoes
  • a handmade card
  • a clean-shaven man
2. … + present participle
word + V-ing
Examples
  • a good-looking boy
  • a tight-fitting dress
  • a time-consuming job
3. … + noun
word + noun
Examples
  • a last-minute change
  • a full-time job
  • a second-hand car
4. … + adjective
word + adjective
Examples
  • a sugar-free drink
  • a brand new phone
  • an eco-friendly bag
5. … + preposition / particle
V3 + up / out / down …
Examples
  • a worn-out carpet
  • rolled-up sleeves
  • a run-down hotel
✍ Hyphen BEFORE a noun…

Many compound adjectives take a hyphen when they come before a noun.

  • a well-known band
  • a worn-out carpet
  • a run-down hotel
…but NOT after a verb

After a verb, they are usually written without a hyphen.

  • The band is well known.
  • The carpet is worn out.
  • The hotel is run down.
💡 Rule of thumb: hyphen before a noun, none after a verb.
🔢 Bonus: compounds with numbers

The noun inside the compound stays singular — even for numbers above one.

✗ a five-years-old boy
✓ a five-year-old boy  (but: The boy is five years old.)
✗ a ten-minutes walk
✓ a ten-minute walk  (but: The walk takes ten minutes.)

Adjective Suffixes: -al · -able · -ive · -ant

We can make adjectives by adding suffixes to nouns or verbs. Learning the suffixes helps you recognise — and build — new words.

-AL

The most common adjective suffix. Mainly formed from nouns.

noun + -al
Examples
  • accident → accidental
  • virus → viral
  • nation → national
  • culture → cultural
  • More: actual, mental
-ABLE

Mainly added to or formed from verbs.

verb + -able
Examples
  • drink → drinkable
  • read → readable
  • rely → reliable
  • More: unmanageable
💡 -able is a "living suffix": we can add it to almost any existing verb — even new ones, e.g. to google → googleable.
-IVE

Mainly added to or formed from verbs.

verb + -ive
Examples
  • select → selective
  • inform → informative
  • create → creative
  • More: addictive, objective
-ANT

Mainly added to or formed from verbs.

verb + -ant
Examples
  • dominate → dominant
  • tolerate → tolerant
  • resist → resistant
  • More: relevant
✍ Watch the spelling
  • Drop a silent -e: dominate → dominant, create → creative
  • Change y → i: rely → reliable
  • Always check new words in a dictionary — spelling can change.
✏️ Try it: use the correct form
SentenceAnswer

Understanding Complex Sentences

Unlock the code

To understand a sentence you need to quickly identify the subject, verb and object. Certain features of long sentences make this difficult — here is how to “unlock the code”.

1. Find the core: Subject – Verb – Object
SUBJECTwho / what does itVERBthe action or stateOBJECTwho / what receives itEXTRAcan be skipped at first
The site publishes serious news.
💡 Every long sentence has a short core like this. Your job is to find it first, then add the extra details.
2. Three features that make long sentences hard

a   Present or past participle clauses

First founded in the US in 2006, BuzzFeed now has branches in several countries, including France, Germany and Brazil.
How to read it
  • First founded in the US in 2006 — a past participle clause: background information (= BuzzFeed was first founded…)
  • including France, Germany and Brazil — a present participle clause: adds examples
💡 Skip the participle clauses → BuzzFeed now has branches.

b   Relative clauses

The site, which used to publish only entertaining lists, now publishes serious news, too.
How to read it
  • which used to publish only entertaining lists — a relative clause that sits between the subject and the verb, so they end up far apart
💡 Skip the clause between the commas → The site now publishes serious news.

c   Subjects or objects consisting of several words

BuzzFeed provides a fun, easy-to-read alternative to more serious news items.
How to read it
  • The object is a long noun phrase. Find the main noun (alternative) — the words before it describe it
  • to more serious news items adds extra information after it
💡 Core sentence → BuzzFeed provides an alternative.
3. Four types of sentence
TypeStructureExample
Simple1 clauseThe company grew.
Compound2 main clauses joined by and, but, or, soThe company grew, but profits fell.
Complexmain clause + subordinate clauseThe company grew because demand increased.
Compound-complex2+ main clauses + a subordinate clauseThe company grew because demand increased, but profits fell.
4. Main clause vs subordinate clause
MAIN clause

Makes complete sense on its own.

Subject + verb (+ object)
  • We went out.
  • The study suggests a link.
SUBORDINATE clause

Cannot stand alone. It starts with a connector and depends on the main clause.

  • Although it was raining, we went out.
💡 Read it alone and it feels unfinished: Although it was raining… → and then?
5. Connectors — signals that a clause starts
JobConnectorsExample
Timewhen · while · as soon as · before · after · untilWhen the bell rang, the students left.
Reasonbecause · since · asShe stayed home because she was ill.
Contrastalthough · even though · whereasAlthough it was cold, we swam.
Conditionif · unless · provided thatUnless you hurry, you'll miss the bus.
Purpose / resultso that · in order to · so … that · such … thatHe spoke slowly so that everyone could follow.
Relativewho · which · that · whose · whereThe man who called is my boss.
Noun clausethat · what · why · how · if · whetherI don't know what she wants.
6. Clauses that give extra information (more detail)
Defining relative clause (no commas)

Tells us which person or thing — essential information.

  • The student who won the prize is my cousin.
💡 If you remove it, the meaning is unclear (which student?).
Non-defining relative clause (commas)

Adds extra information — it can be removed.

  • My cousin, who lives in Berlin, works for a bank.
💡 Remove the part between the commas — the sentence still works.
-ing clause (active)

= who / which + verb

  • The man standing at the door is my uncle.
  • (= who is standing at the door)
-ed clause (passive)

= which was + V3

  • The report published last week was criticised.
  • (= which was published last week)
Clause at the start

Gives time or reason.

  • Built in 1990, the bridge needs repair.
  • Having finished the report, she went home.
Noun clauses

A whole clause that works like a noun (subject or object).

  • I don't know what she wants. (object)
  • Whether we can afford it is unclear. (subject)
✗ I don't know what does she want.
✓ I don't know what she wants. (statement word order)
Inserted information

Extra details can sit inside commas, dashes or brackets.

  • The report — which took six months to write — was ignored.
  • Dr Karimova, the head of the department, announced the changes.
💡 Skip it on the first reading, then come back to it.
7. Five steps to decode any long sentence
StepWhat to doAsk yourself
1Find the main verb, its subject and its objectWho or what does what?
2Spot the connectors (because, although, who, which, that…)Where does a new clause start?
3Put [brackets] around each subordinate clauseWhat does it add: time, reason, contrast, condition?
4Skip extra information: participle clauses, relative clauses, commas, dashes, bracketsDoes the sentence still work without it?
5Rebuild the meaningWho did what, when, why — in spite of what?
🔬 Worked Example
The study, which was carried out by researchers at three universities, suggests that people who sleep less than six hours a night are more likely to develop heart disease, although the link is not yet fully understood.

🚩 Common Traps
Watch out!

1. A long subject, far from its verb

  • The results of the experiments carried out in three countries were surprising. (subject = results → were)

2. "that" has two jobs

  • She said that she was tired. (conjunction: starts a noun clause)
  • The book that I borrowed was long. (relative pronoun)

3. One connector, two meanings

  • Since 2010, he has lived here. (time)
  • Since it was late, we left. (reason)
