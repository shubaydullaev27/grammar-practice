Why teenagers need to sleep in

Anyone who has tried to wake a teenager at half past six in the morning knows how difficult it can be. Parents often blame laziness, but scientists say the real explanation is biological. During adolescence the body’s internal clock shifts, so that teenagers naturally feel sleepy later at night and want to wake up later in the morning. Most experts agree that teenagers need between eight and ten hours of sleep, yet many get far less.

This mismatch matters because many schools start very early. In some districts in the United States, lessons begin before 7.30 a.m., which means that students must get up before dawn. Research has linked this pattern to a range of problems, including poor concentration, low mood and a higher risk of accidents among young drivers. Some researchers have also found a connection between lack of sleep and weight gain, since tired teenagers tend to choose sugary snacks for quick energy.

A number of schools have decided to experiment. When schools in Seattle moved their start time from about 7.50 to 8.45 a.m. in 2016, a study found that students slept around half an hour more each night, and their attendance and grades improved slightly. Similar changes elsewhere have led to fewer students arriving late and fewer visits to the school nurse.

Not everyone is convinced. Later starts also mean later finishes, which can disrupt after-school sports and part-time jobs. Working parents may find it hard to arrange childcare, and bus companies warn that changing timetables could be expensive. Some critics also suggest that teenagers will simply stay up later if they know they can sleep in, so the benefits may disappear.

Nevertheless, many doctors’ organisations now recommend that secondary schools begin no earlier than 8.30 a.m. The debate shows that education policy is not only about what students learn but also about when their brains are ready to learn it.
