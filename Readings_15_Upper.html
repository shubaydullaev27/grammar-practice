Reading 1

Working from anywhere

A Before 2020, working from home was a privilege granted to a small number of employees, usually after a long negotiation with their managers. Then, almost overnight, millions of people were sent home with their laptops, and companies discovered that many jobs could be done perfectly well outside the office.

B Surveys suggest that most employees now prefer some form of flexibility. They save hours of commuting every week, spend more time with their families and can arrange the day around their most productive hours. Many employers have also noticed that they can recruit talented people from other cities, or even other countries, rather than only from the area around their offices.

C Technology has made this shift possible. Cloud software allows colleagues to edit the same document at the same time, and video platforms have replaced many meetings that once required a flight or a long drive. Some companies have gone further and closed their headquarters altogether, allowing employees to live wherever they choose. For people in rural areas, or those with a disability that makes travelling difficult, this has opened opportunities that barely existed a decade ago.

D However, the picture is not entirely positive. Some remote workers report feeling isolated, particularly younger employees who have not yet built relationships with colleagues. The boundary between work and leisure can also disappear, so people answer emails late in the evening and find it difficult to switch off. In addition, informal conversations at the coffee machine, which often produce new ideas, are hard to reproduce on a video call.

E As a result, many companies have chosen a hybrid model, in which staff spend two or three days in the office and the rest at home. Supporters say this combines the advantages of both systems. Critics argue that it can be unfair, because employees who are present in the office may be noticed and promoted more often than those who stay at home.

F The effect on cities has been dramatic. Cafés and sandwich shops that depended on office workers at lunchtime have lost customers, and some city centres feel noticeably quieter on Mondays and Fridays. Property owners are asking whether so many large office buildings are still needed, and a few are being converted into apartments. At the same time, suburbs and small towns have gained new life, as people who no longer commute spend more money in their local shops.

G Whatever the final pattern, it seems unlikely that the traditional nine-to-five office week will return in its old form. The challenge for managers is to judge people by what they achieve, not by how many hours they are visible at a desk.

Why do we dream?

A Every night, most people experience several dreams, although they usually remember only a few of them, and often only for a few minutes after waking. Scientists have studied dreaming for more than a century, yet they still disagree about its purpose.

B Most vivid dreams occur during a phase of sleep known as REM, or rapid eye movement, when the brain is almost as active as it is during the day. One popular theory suggests that dreaming helps the brain to process memories. During the night, important experiences are strengthened and unimportant details are discarded, and dreams may be a side effect of this sorting process.

C The idea has a long history. Ancient civilisations regarded dreams as messages from the gods, and rulers often kept advisers whose job was to interpret them. In the early twentieth century, Sigmund Freud argued that dreams reveal hidden wishes that the mind cannot admit when awake. Few scientists accept his theory today, but it encouraged researchers to take dreams seriously as a subject of study, and it shaped the way many people still talk about them.

D Another theory focuses on emotions. Researchers have found that people who are woken during REM sleep find it harder to cope with stressful events the following day. According to this view, dreams work like a form of overnight therapy: they allow us to revisit upsetting situations in a safe environment, so that the feelings attached to them become weaker.

E A third group of scientists is more sceptical. They argue that dreams have no function at all and are simply random signals created by a resting brain, which the mind then turns into a story. Evidence for this idea is limited, but it is difficult to disprove.

F Modern technology is giving researchers new tools. Brain scanners can now show which regions become active while a person sleeps, and in some experiments scientists have been able to predict, with modest accuracy, the general content of a dream from the pattern of activity. Other teams have trained volunteers to recognise that they are dreaming and to signal this to the researchers by moving their eyes. These studies are small, but they suggest that the private world of dreams may one day be easier to investigate.

G Although the debate continues, nearly everyone agrees on one point: sleep is essential. People who regularly sleep for fewer than six hours are more likely to suffer from poor concentration and a weaker immune system, whether or not they remember their dreams.

Reading 3

The psychology of colour

A Walk into any supermarket and you will see that colour is rarely chosen by accident. Companies spend millions of dollars deciding which shades will make customers feel hungry, relaxed or confident, because many people form an opinion about a product within seconds, and colour is often the main reason.

B Red is a good example. It is linked with energy and excitement, which may explain why it is so common in fast-food restaurants and on sale signs. Blue, on the other hand, is usually associated with calm and trust, and many banks and technology companies include it in their logos. Green suggests health and nature, so it appears on organic food and on products described as environmentally friendly.

C Colour is also a matter of cost and technology. For centuries, the pigments used by painters and dyers came from plants, insects and minerals, and a few shades were so rare that they were worth more than gold. The invention of synthetic dyes in the nineteenth century changed this, making bright clothes available to ordinary people for the first time. Today, designers choose from thousands of shades, which increases the importance of choosing the right one.

D However, researchers warn that these associations are not universal. In many Western countries white is connected with weddings and purity, while in parts of East Asia it is traditionally worn at funerals. Similarly, purple was once so expensive to produce that only royalty could wear it, but today it has lost much of that status.

E Colour also affects behaviour in less obvious ways. Studies of classrooms suggest that warm, bright walls can make children more energetic, whereas softer shades help them to concentrate. Some hospitals have repainted waiting rooms in pale greens and blues in an attempt to reduce patients’ anxiety, although the evidence that this works is mixed.

F Sport offers another example. Some studies of competitions have suggested that athletes wearing red win slightly more often than those in blue, possibly because red is linked with aggression, although other researchers have been unable to repeat the result. Such findings show how difficult it is to separate the effect of colour from other factors, such as skill and preparation. Scientists therefore treat dramatic claims about colour with caution until they have been confirmed more than once.

G Designers therefore need to be careful. A colour that creates the right feeling in one culture may send the wrong message in another, and personal experience can matter as much as general rules.

Bees in the city

A Beekeeping is no longer a hobby reserved for the countryside. In recent years, thousands of hives have appeared on the roofs of offices, hotels and apartment buildings in cities such as London, Paris and New York. The trend began as a way of helping bees, whose numbers have fallen in many regions because of disease, pesticides and the loss of wild flowers.

B Surprisingly, cities can be a good home for bees. Farmland often contains vast fields of a single crop, which offers food for only a few weeks, whereas parks, gardens and balconies provide a mixture of flowers that bloom at different times of the year. Urban areas also tend to use fewer pesticides than intensive agriculture, and temperatures are slightly higher, which allows bees to start foraging earlier in spring.

C The idea is not entirely new. Keeping bees in towns was common in earlier centuries, when many households relied on honey as their main sweetener and on wax for candles. The practice declined as sugar became cheap and cities grew more crowded, but the recent revival has been helped by training courses, online communities and clubs where beginners can learn from experienced keepers. Many newcomers say that the hobby has taught them patience as well as biology.

D Urban honey has become popular as well. Because the bees collect nectar from many different trees and plants, each hive produces a flavour that reflects its neighbourhood, and some restaurants now serve honey from their own roofs. Beekeepers say that this connects city residents with the natural world, and many companies use hives as part of their environmental programmes.

E Not everyone is convinced, however. Some ecologists warn that there may now be too many honeybees in certain cities, and that they compete for flowers with wild species such as bumblebees, many of which are more at risk. In their view, planting more flowers and protecting natural habitats would help pollinators more than adding hives.

F Another difficulty is public attitude. Some residents worry about stings, and landlords occasionally refuse permission for hives on their buildings. Experienced keepers answer that honeybees are rarely aggressive away from the hive, and that a well-managed colony poses little danger to neighbours. They add that education matters, because people who understand how bees behave tend to feel less anxious about them.

G The debate has encouraged a new approach. Instead of simply keeping more bees, some cities are creating pollinator corridors, which are connected strips of flowers that allow all kinds of insects to travel safely across the urban landscape.

Reading 5

Small loans, big hopes

A In 1976, an economist in Bangladesh lent a small amount of money to a group of village women who made furniture from bamboo. The sum was tiny, but it allowed them to buy materials without paying high interest to local moneylenders. The experiment grew into a bank, and its founder later received the Nobel Peace Prize. The idea he popularised is known as microfinance.

B Microfinance offers small loans, savings accounts and sometimes insurance to people who are too poor to use ordinary banks. Traditional banks usually refuse such customers because they have no property to offer as security, and the cost of managing a small loan is high compared with the profit. Microfinance organisations solve this problem by lending to groups. Each member is responsible for the others, so social pressure encourages everyone to repay.

C Another feature is the way repayment is organised. Meetings are often held every week in the village, so that loan officers meet borrowers in person and small problems can be solved before they become serious. The system relies on trust, which explains why repayment rates have often been high, even though the borrowers are much poorer than the customers of conventional lenders.

D The results have often been encouraging. Borrowers use their loans to buy a sewing machine, a cow or stock for a market stall, and the extra income can pay for food, medicine and school fees. Women, who receive the majority of the loans, often repay more reliably than men, and families with a woman who earns money frequently invest more in their children’s education.

E Nevertheless, microfinance is not a miracle cure. Studies by independent researchers have found that, on average, loans increase business activity but do not always lift families out of poverty. Interest rates can be high, because small loans are expensive to manage, and some borrowers have fallen into debt by taking out new loans to repay old ones. Critics also point out that many people are too poor or too ill to start a business at all.

F In recent years the model has changed. Many organisations now offer services through mobile phones, so that customers can save, send money and pay bills without travelling to a branch. This is especially valuable for people in remote areas, and it lowers the cost of serving them. Some critics worry that digital lending can encourage people to borrow too easily, which is why many experts call for clear rules and honest information about interest rates.

G As a result, supporters now argue that microfinance works best as part of a wider system that includes training, healthcare and fair regulation of lenders.

Reading 6

The future of coral reefs

A Coral reefs cover less than one per cent of the ocean floor, yet they support roughly a quarter of all marine species. Often described as the rainforests of the sea, they provide food and shelter for fish, protect coastlines from storms and attract millions of tourists every year.

B A reef is built by tiny animals called polyps, which live in partnership with algae. The algae live inside the coral’s tissue and use sunlight to produce food, giving the coral its bright colours. This relationship is delicate. When the water becomes too warm, the coral expels the algae, turns white and, if the heat continues, eventually starves. This process is known as bleaching.

C The loss of a reef affects people as well as animals. Millions of families, particularly in small island nations, depend on reef fish for food and income, and tourism linked to diving and snorkelling is a major source of jobs. Healthy reefs also act as natural barriers, absorbing much of the energy of waves before they reach the shore. Where reefs have died, coastal communities have found that storms cause more damage and that rebuilding sea defences is extremely expensive.

D Climate change is the greatest threat, because the oceans have absorbed most of the extra heat created by greenhouse gases. Several severe bleaching events have damaged large areas of reef in the past decade. However, other pressures make matters worse. Fertilisers washed from farms feed algae that smother the coral, while overfishing removes the fish that keep the reef clean, and some fishermen still use destructive methods such as dynamite.

E Scientists are experimenting with ways of helping reefs survive. In some places, volunteers grow fragments of coral in underwater nurseries and attach them to damaged areas. Others are trying to breed varieties that tolerate higher temperatures. These projects have produced encouraging results on a small scale, but experts stress that they cannot replace action on the underlying problem.

F Governments have started to respond in various ways. Some have created marine parks where fishing is limited and boats must keep to certain areas, and these protected zones have often recovered faster than unprotected ones. Others have banned sunscreens containing chemicals that may harm coral, although scientists continue to debate how serious that problem is compared with warming. Whatever the measures, specialists agree that they work best when local people are involved in the decisions.

G Without a rapid reduction in carbon emissions, many researchers fear that most of the world’s reefs could be lost by the end of the century.

Languages in danger

A There are roughly seven thousand languages in the world, but linguists estimate that about half of them are endangered. On average, one language is thought to disappear every few weeks, usually when its last fluent speaker dies without having passed it on to the next generation.

B Most endangered languages are spoken by small communities in places such as Papua New Guinea, Indonesia and the Amazon basin. The reasons for their decline are rarely dramatic. Young people move to cities to find work and discover that success depends on a dominant language such as English, Spanish or Mandarin. Parents, wanting the best for their children, often stop speaking the community language at home, and within two generations it has vanished.

C Historical forces explain much of this loss. In many countries, children were punished for speaking their home language at school, and families concluded that it was safer for their children to use only the national language. In other places, people were moved from their land, which broke up communities that had spoken the same language for centuries. Today the pressure is more likely to come from television, the internet and economic opportunity than from official policy.

D Why should this matter? Linguists point out that every language contains a unique way of describing the world. Some languages have detailed vocabularies for local plants, animals and weather that science has not yet recorded, and when the language dies, this knowledge may disappear with it. Language is also closely tied to identity, so losing it can weaken a community’s sense of belonging.

E Some communities have succeeded in reversing the trend. Maori in New Zealand and Welsh in Britain were once in serious decline, but language nests for young children, bilingual schools and television channels have encouraged new speakers. Technology can help as well: apps, online dictionaries and recorded stories allow elders to share their language with young people living far away.

F Researchers are also racing to document languages that cannot be saved. Linguists record elderly speakers telling stories, singing songs and explaining everyday tasks, and they store the files in digital archives that future generations can use. The work is slow and requires the trust of the community, since the words belong to the people who speak them. Many linguists now say that they should work with communities, not simply study them.

G Revival is never easy, and it requires commitment from governments as well as families. Nevertheless, these examples show that decline is not always inevitable.

Reading 8

Artificial intelligence in medicine

A Doctors have always depended on pattern recognition. After years of training, a radiologist can look at an X-ray and notice small changes that suggest disease. Today, computer programs trained on millions of images are beginning to do the same task, and in some studies they detect certain cancers as accurately as experienced specialists.

B The advantages are clear. A computer does not get tired, and it can examine thousands of scans in the time it takes a person to examine a handful. In regions where specialists are scarce, such software could allow a nurse to screen patients and send only the most worrying cases to a hospital. Artificial intelligence is also being used to predict which patients are most likely to develop complications, so that treatment can begin earlier.

C Beyond imaging, the same techniques are being tested in many other areas. Programs can read the results of blood tests, scan medical records for early warning signs and suggest which medicines are most likely to suit a particular patient. Drug companies use similar tools to search millions of chemical compounds, hoping to find new treatments more quickly and at lower cost than traditional laboratory methods. Some of these projects have already produced promising candidates for further testing.

D Nevertheless, doctors urge caution. A program is only as good as the data on which it was trained. If most of the images come from one group of patients, the system may perform less well on people from other backgrounds. There are also questions about responsibility: if an algorithm makes a mistake, it is not clear whether the blame lies with the doctor, the hospital or the company that built the software.

E Many patients are uneasy as well. Surveys show that people are generally happy for computers to assist their doctors but are reluctant to accept a diagnosis delivered by a machine alone. They value the explanation, reassurance and human contact that a doctor provides, qualities that software cannot easily imitate.

F Privacy is another concern. Training a system requires large quantities of personal medical information, and patients may worry about who can see it and how it might be used. Governments have introduced laws that limit the sharing of health data, but critics say that technology is developing faster than regulation. Hospitals that wish to use these tools must therefore persuade patients that their information will be protected.

G For this reason, most experts expect AI to become a powerful assistant rather than a replacement. The doctors of the future may spend less time examining images and more time talking to patients.

Is less really more?

A A growing number of people are choosing to own fewer things. Known as minimalism, the movement encourages people to keep only possessions that are useful or that they truly love, and to remove everything else. Its supporters say that a simpler home leads to a calmer mind.

B The idea is partly a reaction to modern consumer culture. Cheap goods and online shopping mean that it is easier than ever to buy things, and the average household now contains far more objects than in the past. Many of them are rarely used and simply collect dust. Minimalists claim that clutter is not just untidy but tiring, because every object competes for our attention and creates a small decision to be made.

C The movement has roots in older traditions. Many religions have taught that happiness does not depend on possessions, and Japanese ideas about tidying and simple design have influenced many Western followers. Books and television programmes about decluttering have made the idea popular, and some people now describe the experience of clearing a wardrobe as emotional as well as practical.

D Followers often describe benefits that go beyond the home. Spending less allows them to save money, work fewer hours or travel, and some report feeling less anxious about status. Others mention an environmental advantage: if people buy fewer new things, fewer resources are used in manufacturing and less waste ends up in landfill.

E Critics, however, say that minimalism can become another kind of consumption. Some enthusiasts spend large sums on expensive furniture in neutral colours, which seems to contradict the philosophy. It may also be a lifestyle available mainly to those who can afford to replace items easily, since a person with limited income cannot always throw away a tool or a coat on the assumption that they can buy another later.

F Putting the philosophy into practice can be harder than it sounds. Many people find that objects carry memories, so that giving away a gift or a child’s drawing feels like a loss. Experts suggest starting with a single drawer or shelf, asking whether each item has been used in the last year, and being patient. Families may also need to negotiate, since one person’s clutter may be another’s collection.

G Perhaps the most useful lesson is not to own as little as possible, but to think carefully about why we buy. The aim, many suggest, is to make room for what matters, whatever that is for each person.

The problem of noise

A When we think of pollution, we usually imagine smoke, chemicals or plastic. Yet noise can also harm health, and the World Health Organization has described it as one of the main environmental risks in Europe, after air pollution. Traffic, aircraft, building work and loud music are all part of the problem.

B The effects are not limited to irritation. Long-term exposure to loud noise has been linked to high blood pressure, heart disease and disturbed sleep, because the body reacts to sudden sounds even when we are asleep. Children who attend schools under flight paths have been found to read less well than children in quieter areas, since noise makes it harder to concentrate and to hear the teacher.

C Noise is also an issue at work. Factory employees, construction workers and airport staff are often exposed to sound levels that can damage hearing, and ear protection is therefore required by law in many countries. Hearing loss develops gradually and is permanent, so people may not notice the problem until they find it difficult to follow a conversation in a busy room. Doctors encourage everyone to avoid prolonged exposure to loud music through headphones as well.

D Wildlife suffers too. Whales and dolphins rely on sound to communicate and find food, and the noise from ships can interfere with their signals. Some birds in cities have changed the pitch of their songs so that mates can hear them above the traffic.

E Solutions exist, but they cost money. Quieter road surfaces, barriers beside motorways and electric vehicles can reduce traffic noise, while some airports restrict night flights. Town planners can also design buildings so that bedrooms face away from busy roads, and add parks and trees, which absorb some sound and provide places of calm.

F Some cities have introduced creative measures. Quiet zones have been marked on maps so that residents can find parks and squares where noise is limited, and a few places use sensors to measure sound and fine drivers whose vehicles are too loud. Architects are experimenting with materials and shapes that reduce echoes, so that public spaces feel calmer even when many people are present.

G Individuals can play a part by choosing quieter appliances and avoiding unnecessary noise. However, campaigners say that real change will come only when governments treat noise as a serious public health issue, rather than a minor annoyance.

The gig economy

A Order a meal through an app, and within half an hour a rider appears at your door. Book a ride from your phone, and a driver arrives in minutes. These services are part of what is called the gig economy, in which people work on short-term tasks rather than permanent jobs, and companies connect them with customers through digital platforms.

B For many workers, the attraction is freedom. They can choose when to work, which is useful for students, parents and anyone who needs a second income. Some enjoy the independence of being their own boss, and starting is usually quick, with no interviews or long contracts. For businesses, the model reduces costs, since they pay only for the work that is actually done.

C The model has changed the way people think about careers. In the past, a worker might spend decades with a single employer, gradually moving up through a company. Today many people combine several sources of income, such as part-time work, freelance projects and occasional jobs on a platform. Some find this exciting, while others feel anxious about the lack of a stable path.

D However, the disadvantages are significant. Most gig workers are classed as self-employed, so they are not entitled to paid holidays, sick pay or a pension. Income can be unpredictable, because demand changes from day to day, and workers must pay for their own equipment, fuel and repairs. Some studies have found that, after these costs are deducted, earnings can fall below the minimum wage.

E Disputes about status have reached the courts. Workers argue that because the platform sets prices and monitors their performance, they behave more like employees than independent contractors. Some courts have agreed, and a few governments have introduced rules requiring platforms to provide basic protections. Companies reply that flexibility would be lost if workers were treated as staff.

F Rating systems are another point of debate. Customers can score workers after each job, and low scores may mean fewer assignments or even removal from the platform. Workers say that this gives them little protection against unfair complaints, while companies argue that ratings help maintain quality. Researchers who study the industry point out that the system also places pressure on workers to be polite and fast at all times.

G The debate is likely to continue as the number of gig workers grows. Finding a balance between flexibility and security is one of the main challenges facing modern labour markets.

Learning that lasts

A Most students have experienced the night-before-the-exam panic: hours of reading, a head full of facts, and a mind that seems to empty a few days later. Psychologists have known for over a century that this kind of cramming is inefficient, and research has identified better ways to learn.

B The first discovery concerns forgetting. In the 1880s, a German psychologist tested his own memory by learning lists of meaningless syllables and found that he forgot most of them within a day or two. The loss follows a curve: it is rapid at first, then gradually slows. However, each time information is reviewed, the curve becomes flatter, meaning that the memory lasts longer.

C Memory is not a single system. Information first enters a short-term store, where it lasts only for seconds unless attention is given to it, and only some of it passes into long-term memory. The more meaningful a fact is, the more likely it is to be kept. A date learned in isolation is quickly lost, whereas the same date linked to a story or a picture is much easier to recall. This is why teachers often encourage students to connect new facts with what they already know.

D This explains why spacing is effective. Studying a subject for twenty minutes on five different days produces better long-term results than studying for one hundred minutes in a single session, even though the total time is identical. Many language-learning apps now use this principle, showing words again just before the learner is likely to forget them.

E A second powerful technique is retrieval practice. Instead of simply re-reading a chapter, students close the book and try to recall what they have learned. The effort feels harder and less comfortable, but it strengthens memory more than passive review. Experiments show that students who test themselves often outperform those who spend the same time re-reading.

F Teachers can apply these ideas in simple ways. Short quizzes at the start of lessons force students to recall earlier material, and homework that returns to old topics helps spread learning over time. Students can make flashcards, explain a topic aloud to a friend or write questions for themselves instead of copying notes. Although these methods require more effort, researchers argue that the effort is exactly what makes them work.

G Finally, sleep matters. During the night, the brain consolidates new information, so a good night’s rest after studying is more useful than staying up late to read again. In short, effective learning is less about working longer than about working in a smarter way.

Turning seawater into drinking water

A More than two billion people live in countries that experience high water stress, and the problem is expected to grow as populations increase and the climate becomes less predictable. Since roughly ninety-seven per cent of the planet’s water is in the oceans, it is not surprising that many countries are turning to desalination, the removal of salt from seawater.

B The most common method today is reverse osmosis, in which seawater is forced through extremely fine membranes that block salt but allow water to pass. Large plants in the Middle East, North Africa and Australia can produce enough drinking water for millions of people, and in a few countries desalination supplies the majority of the public water supply.

C The technology has a long history. Sailors have boiled seawater and collected the steam for centuries, and early plants in the twentieth century used the same principle on a larger scale. Heating water requires so much energy, however, that the method was practical only in a few wealthy, dry places. The introduction of membranes made the process far cheaper and opened the way for the large plants that exist today.

D However, the process has drawbacks. It needs a great deal of energy, which makes the water expensive and, if the electricity comes from fossil fuels, adds to carbon emissions. There is also the problem of brine, the very salty water that remains after treatment. If it is released into the sea without care, it can harm marine life near the outlet.

E Engineers are working on improvements. New membranes need less pressure, and some plants now run on solar power or recover energy from the pressurised water. Researchers are also exploring ways of diluting brine before release, or of extracting useful minerals from it.

F Small-scale solutions are also emerging. In remote villages and on islands, compact units powered by the sun can provide a few hundred litres of clean water a day, enough for a school or a clinic. Such systems are much less efficient than large plants, but they avoid the cost of pipelines and make communities less dependent on deliveries. Aid organisations are testing designs that local technicians can repair with simple tools.

G Even so, many experts argue that desalination should be a last resort and not the first. In most countries, cheaper measures such as repairing leaking pipes, recycling wastewater and encouraging households to use less would save more water at lower cost.

The Silk Road

A The name Silk Road suggests a single highway across Asia, but it was in fact a network of trade routes that connected China with the Mediterranean for well over a thousand years. The term itself is surprisingly recent: it was introduced by a German geographer in the nineteenth century, long after the routes had declined.

B Few merchants travelled the whole distance. Goods were passed from trader to trader, and cities along the way, such as Samarkand, Bukhara and Kashgar, grew rich as markets and resting places for caravans. Silk was only one of many products. Caravans also carried spices, porcelain, glass, horses, metals and precious stones.

C Daily life along the routes was demanding. Caravans crossed deserts and mountain passes where water was scarce, and travellers faced bandits, storms and disease. To protect them, rulers built roadside inns, known as caravanserais, where merchants could rest, store goods and exchange news. Many of these buildings survive today and give visitors a clear picture of how the trade worked.

D Equally important were the ideas that travelled with the goods. Buddhism spread from India into Central and East Asia along these routes, and later Islam moved in the opposite direction. Technologies such as papermaking and gunpowder were transmitted westwards, while crops such as grapes and cotton reached new regions. Travellers also exchanged languages, music and styles of art, so that cities along the Silk Road developed remarkably mixed cultures.

E The network began to decline when sea routes between Europe and Asia became safer and cheaper, and when the political stability that protected caravans broke down. Many oases that had been prosperous for centuries became quiet towns.

F Individual travellers left vivid accounts of what they saw. Pilgrims, diplomats and merchants wrote about unfamiliar foods, customs and religions, and their descriptions often surprised readers at home. Some of these works were copied again and again, and they influenced the way Europeans and Asians imagined each other for centuries. Modern historians use them carefully, since travellers sometimes repeated stories they had heard without checking whether they were true.

G Today, the Silk Road is attracting attention again. Several countries have joined together to nominate heritage sites for international protection, and tourism and new transport projects are bringing visitors to old caravan cities. For historians, however, its greatest lesson is that cultures have always been shaped by contact with each other.

The rise of esports

A A few years ago, the idea that people would pay to watch others play video games seemed strange. Today, esports, or organised competitive gaming, attract audiences that rival those of traditional sports. Major tournaments fill stadiums, are streamed to millions of viewers online, and offer prize pools worth millions of dollars.

B Professional players treat gaming as a full-time career. They often train for eight hours or more a day, analyse recordings of their matches and follow strict routines of exercise and sleep, because quick reactions and concentration decline quickly with tiredness. Most players reach their peak in their early twenties, and many retire from competition before the age of thirty.

C Spectators are drawn by more than the competition itself. Commentators explain tactics in the way that football presenters do, and fans follow their favourite teams and players on social media. Many tournaments also create a festival atmosphere, with music, costumes and opportunities to meet the players. For younger viewers, who may rarely watch traditional television, the online stream has become the natural way to follow a sport.

D Behind the players is a growing industry. Teams recruit coaches, analysts and psychologists, while companies pay for sponsorship and advertising. Universities in several countries now offer scholarships to talented gamers, and some schools have introduced clubs that teach teamwork and strategy.

E Not everyone welcomes the trend. Some parents worry that children will spend too many hours sitting in front of a screen, and doctors warn about eye strain, poor posture and lack of exercise. Critics also question whether gaming should be called a sport at all, since it does not demand the same physical effort as football or athletics. Supporters answer that it requires skill, tactics and fast coordination, and that sport has always been defined by competition rather than sweat.

F Education and health groups are also paying attention. Some researchers suggest that strategy games can improve decision-making and teamwork, while others argue that the evidence is limited and that benefits depend on how much time is spent playing. Parents are advised to agree clear limits and to encourage regular breaks, sleep and physical activity. Many professional teams now employ fitness trainers for exactly this reason.

G There is also a debate about inclusion. Although almost half of all gamers are women, the professional scene is dominated by men, and organisers are trying to make competitions more welcoming. Whatever the final verdict, esports seem likely to keep growing as a new generation of viewers takes its place in the audience.

Exploring the hidden city

A Urban exploration, sometimes shortened to urbex, is the practice of visiting parts of the built environment that are normally closed to the public. These include abandoned hospitals and factories, disused railway stations, rooftops, tunnels and the service corridors beneath large cities. Enthusiasts are usually not looking for danger. What attracts them, they say, is the chance to see a familiar city from an unfamiliar angle and to stand in places where history has been left undisturbed.

B For many explorers, photography is central. An empty ballroom with peeling paint, a school where books still lie open on desks, or a power station overgrown with plants can make powerful images, and online galleries have created an audience for them. Others are more interested in research than pictures. They study old maps and newspaper archives before a visit, and some publish detailed accounts of the buildings they have found. Whatever their motive, most agree that the first impression of such a place is hard to forget.

C The risks, however, are considerable. Floors in neglected buildings may be rotten, staircases can collapse without warning, and old industrial sites may contain asbestos or other hazardous substances. Underground, the danger of flooding or poor air quality is real, and a person who is injured in an unknown location may wait a long time for help. Experienced explorers therefore never go alone, carry torches and first-aid equipment, and tell a friend where they have gone.

D There is also the question of the law. In most countries, entering a building without permission counts as trespass, and some sites are protected because they are unsafe or sensitive. Explorers sometimes receive fines, and a few have been prosecuted for damage caused by others who followed them. For this reason, many follow an unwritten code: do not break in, do not remove objects and do not reveal exact locations, since publicity can attract vandals and thieves.

E Cities and owners have responded in different ways. Some have strengthened fences and alarms, while others have recognised the public interest in hidden places and organised guided visits to tunnels, bunkers and old factories under proper supervision. These tours are safer and legal, and they raise money for repairs, although many explorers say they miss the sense of discovery that comes with finding a place alone.

F Supporters argue that urban exploration encourages people to value buildings that would otherwise be forgotten, and that photographs sometimes record places shortly before they are demolished. Critics reply that the hobby puts people at risk and can harm fragile sites. Perhaps the fairest conclusion is that its value depends on how it is practised: with respect, preparation and a willingness to leave a place exactly as it was found.
