Direct & Indirect Questions

Question Word Order

Two ways to ask the same thing — one direct, one polite. The word order is what changes.

Direct Questions

These are normal questions.

Question word + Auxiliary + Subject + Verb
Examples
  • Where is the nearest post office?
  • Why are you late?
  • When did you visit New York City?
  • Who is that person?
Indirect Questions

These are more polite questions.

Common beginnings: Do you know… / Could you tell me… / Can you tell me… / Would you mind telling me… / I'd like to know… / I wonder…

Introductory phrase + Question word + Subject + Verb
⚠️ Do NOT use question word order after the question word.
⚖️ Compare
DIRECTINDIRECT
Where is the nearest post office?Do you know where the nearest post office is?
Why are you late?Could you explain why you are late?
Who is that person over there?Would you mind telling me who that person over there is?
When did you last visit New York City?Can you tell me when you last visited New York City?
⚠️ Notice: did visit → visited — no auxiliary (do/does/did) in the indirect question.

First Conditional

if / unless / will / won't

Use it for possible future situations.

Structure
If + Present Simple, will / won't + verb
Examples
  • If it rains, we will stay home.
  • If you study, you will pass.
  • If she doesn't call, I won't wait.
IF = “in case”

Use if for a normal condition.

Examples
  • If it rains tomorrow, we will have to postpone the picnic.
  • If you hurry, you will catch the bus.
  • If I see him, I will tell him.
✗ If it rains tomorrow, we won't have to postpone the picnic.
✓ If it rains tomorrow, we will have to postpone the picnic.
💡 Rain causes the picnic to be postponed.
UNLESS = “if not”

Unless = if … not

Compare:

  • If we don't leave now, we won't make it on time.
  • Unless we leave now, we won't make it on time. (same meaning)
Unless + Present Simple, will / won't + verb
  • Unless you study, you won't pass.
  • Unless she arrives soon, we will start without her.
  • Unless we take action now, climate change will continue to worsen.
🚩 Common Exam Traps

1. "Unless" already contains NOT

✗ Unless we don't leave now…
✓ Unless we leave now…

2. Think about the result

Unless we leave now, we won't make it to the movie on time.

Meaning: If we do not leave now → we will be late. The result is negative → won't.

TOPIC 3 OF 9

Passive Voice

Passive voice is used when the action is more important than the person who does it.

Active
  • The committee chose a new leader.
Passive
  • A new leader was chosen (by the committee).
Structure
Object + be + Past Participle (V3) + (by + agent)

The agent (by…) is often omitted if it is unknown or unimportant.

Present Simple Passive
am / is / are + V3
Examples
  • English is spoken in many countries.
  • The rooms are cleaned every day.
  • Coffee is grown in Brazil.
Past Simple Passive
was / were + V3
Examples
  • The bridge was built in 2010.
  • The match was cancelled yesterday.
  • A new leader was chosen last week.
Present Continuous Passive
am / is / are + being + V3
Examples
  • The reports are being checked now.
  • The road is being repaired.
  • Dinner is being prepared.
Past Continuous Passive
was / were + being + V3
Examples
  • The house was being painted yesterday.
  • The students were being tested.
  • The road was being repaired when we arrived.
Present Perfect Passive
have / has + been + V3
Examples
  • The time of the concert has been changed.
  • The work has been finished.
  • The documents have been sent.
Future Simple Passive
will be + V3
Examples
  • The results will be announced tomorrow.
  • The meeting will be held next week.
  • The package will be delivered soon.
Passive with Modals
Modal + be + V3
  • The documents must be submitted before the deadline.
  • Homework should be finished today.
  • This problem can be solved.
  • The form has to be completed.
  • Mobile phones mustn't be used here.

TOPIC 4 OF 9

Second Conditional

Use the Second Conditional to talk about imaginary or unlikely situations in the present or future, give advice, or describe dreams and hypothetical situations.

Structure
If + Past Simple, ... would + V1

OR: Would + V1 ... if + Past Simple

Examples
  • If I had more money, I would buy a new car.
  • If she studied harder, she would pass the exam.
  • We would travel more if we had more free time.
‘Were’ instead of ‘Was’

With the verb be, we usually use were for all subjects in formal English.

  • If I were you, I'd apologise.
  • If he were taller, he would play basketball.
💡 In informal English, "was" is sometimes heard, but exams prefer were.
🔹 Common Uses
1. Imaginary Situations
  • If I lived in Paris, I would speak French every day.
💡 But I don't live there.
2. Unlikely Situations
  • If it snowed in July, everyone would be surprised.
💡 Very unlikely.
3. Giving Advice

If I were you, I'd…

  • If I were you, I'd talk to the teacher.
  • If I were you, I wouldn't quit my job.
  • If I were you, I'd take the opportunity.

TOPIC 5 OF 9

Third Conditional

Use the Third Conditional to talk about imaginary past situations, things that didn't happen, regrets, criticism, or a different past result. The past cannot be changed.

When do we use it?
  • imaginary past situations
  • things that didn't happen
  • regrets
  • criticism
  • imagining a different past result
Structure
If + Past Perfect, ... would have + V3

OR: Would have + V3 ... if + Past Perfect

Common Uses

1. Regret: If I had listened to my parents, I would have made a better decision.

2. Imaginary past result: If we had booked earlier, we would have found cheaper tickets.

3. Criticism: If you had been more careful, you wouldn't have broken your phone.

✅ Using WOULD HAVE

would have + V3 = imagined past result

  • If I had studied harder, I would have passed.
  • If they had left earlier, they would have caught the train.
  • She would have accepted the offer if she had known.
✅ Using COULD HAVE

could have + V3 = past ability or possibility

  • If I had had more money, I could have bought the car.
  • If we had arrived earlier, we could have met the manager.
  • If she had trained harder, she could have won.
💡 Meaning: it was possible, but it didn't happen.
✅ Using MIGHT HAVE

might have + V3 = uncertain result

  • If you had called, I might have answered.
  • If it hadn't rained, we might have gone to the beach.
  • If he had accepted the job, he might have been happier.
💡 Meaning: maybe yes, maybe no.
✅ Using COULDN'T HAVE

couldn't have + V3 = impossible

  • If I hadn't brought my passport, I couldn't have travelled.
  • If she hadn't helped us, we couldn't have finished.

Reported Speech

We use Reported Speech to tell someone what another person said.

What is Reported Speech?

Direct Speech: Tom said, "I am tired."

Reported Speech: Tom said (that) he was tired.

💡 "That" is optional.
Structure
Subject + said (that) + reported sentence

OR: Subject + told + person + reported sentence

  • She said (that) she was busy.
  • She told me (that) she was busy.
⚠️ Say → no object. Tell → needs an object.
✗ He told he was tired.
✓ He said he was tired. / He told me he was tired.
🔹 Backshift of Tenses

When the reporting verb is in the past (said, told), the tense usually moves one step back.

🔹 Orders & Requests — to / not to

To report an order or request we use a to-infinitive. We do not backshift tenses here.

Positive orders
told / asked + person + to + V1
  • "Close the door!" → She told me to close the door.
  • "Please help me." → He asked me to help him.
Orders starting with Don't
told / asked + person + not to + V1
  • "Don't touch that!" → He told me not to touch it.
  • "Don't be late." → She told us not to be late.
✗ He told me don't touch it.
✓ He told me not to touch it.
💡 Don't + V1 → not to + V1

📅 previous / before vs next / following

For past times we use the … before or the previous …. For future times we use the next … or the following ….

  • "I saw him last week." → She said she had seen him the previous week (= the week before).
  • "We're leaving next month." → He said they were leaving the following month.

Reported Questions

We use Reported Questions to report what someone asked.

What are Reported Questions?

Direct: "Where do you live?"

Reported: He asked where I lived.

💡 We are reporting a question, not asking one.
Step 1 — Identify the Type of Question
Wh- Questions

Keep the question word (what, where, when, why, who, how…).

asked + Question word + Subject + Verb
  • "Where do you live?" → He asked where I lived.
  • "Why were you late?" → The teacher asked why I had been late.
Yes/No Questions

Use if or whether.

asked + if / whether + Subject + Verb
  • "Are you busy?" → He asked if I was busy.
  • "Can you swim?" → She asked whether I could swim.
Step 2 — Change the Word Order
Use statement word order in reported questions.

Direct: Question word + Auxiliary + Subject + Verb — Where do you live?

Reported: Question word + Subject + Verb

✗ He asked where did I live.
✓ He asked where I lived.

Comparatives & Superlatives

We use comparatives to compare two people or things, and superlatives to compare one person or thing with everything in its group.

🔎 Five Key Sentences
SentenceWhat it shows
1 George is a banker. He's richer than most of his friends.comparative: adjective + -er + than
2 Living in London is more expensive than living in Birmingham.comparative: more + long adjective + than
3 Some people think that watching golf is less exciting than watching football.negative comparison: less + adjective + than
4 It's the best meal I've ever had.superlative: the + best (irregular)
5 The Pyramids in Egypt are some of the oldest buildings in the world.superlative: the + adjective + -est
Comparatives

We use the comparative form of adjectives to compare people and things with each other.

  • I'm taller than my sisters.
  • Paris is more beautiful than London.
1-syllable adjectives

Add -er (or -r if the adjective ends in -e).

adjective + -er
  • short → shorter
Multi-syllable adjectives

Use more before the adjective.

more + adjective
  • ancient → more ancient
Some 2-syllable adjectives

We can either add -er or use more.

  • gentle → gentler / more gentle
2-syllable adjectives ending in -y

Change -y to i and add -er.

  • happy → happier
Negative comparison

Use less + adjective.

less + adjective + than
  • Beards are less popular than they used to be.
Direct comparison: than

We use than when we make a direct comparison between two things.

comparative + than
  • Pedro's hair is darker than Lucca's.
Same or not the same: (just) as … as

To say something is the same, we use (just) as … as. To say something is not the same, we use not as / so … as.

(just) + as + adjective + as | not as / so + adjective + as

just is optional — it makes the meaning stronger (= exactly the same). It always comes before the first “as”.

  • I'm as tall as my dad.
  • I'm just as tall as my dad. (same meaning, stronger)
  • Paola's hair is not as long as before.
✗ She is as just tall as me.
✓ She is just as tall as me.
💡 Word order: (just) → as → adjective → as.
🎚 Modifiers — how big is the difference?

We use modifiers when we want to give more detail about the degree of difference between two things.

  • I'm a bit taller than my brother.
  • Anna is much older than Andre.
Stronger difference

a lot · far · much · so much · very much

  • Anna is much older than Andre.
Smaller difference

a bit · a little · slightly

  • I'm a bit taller than my brother.
No difference

not any · no

  • The new phone isn't any better than the old one.
  • It's no better than before.
Superlatives

We use superlatives to compare people and things with everything in their group.

  • Alex is the tallest person I know.
  • She's the most intelligent person I've ever met.
1-syllable & most 2-syllable adjectives

Use the and add -est.

the + adjective + -est
  • short → the shortest
Multi-syllable adjectives

Use the most.

the most + adjective
  • beautiful → the most beautiful
📋 Summary
Type of adjectiveComparativeSuperlative
1 syllable-er (short → shorter)the -est (the shortest)
2 syllables ending in -y-ier (happy → happier)the -iest (the happiest)
Other 2 syllables-er or more (gentle → gentler / more gentle)the -est or the most (the gentlest / the most gentle)
3+ syllablesmore + adjective (more beautiful)the most + adjective (the most beautiful)
Negativeless + adjective (less popular)the least + adjective (the least popular)

⚠ Common Mistakes
Don't double up
✗ She is more taller than me.
✓ She is taller than me.
✗ He is the most tallest boy.
✓ He is the tallest boy.
than vs as
✗ My car is faster as yours.
✓ My car is faster than yours.
✗ He is as tall than his dad.
✓ He is as tall as his dad.

TOPIC 9 OF 9

should / shouldn't have

We use should have or shouldn't have + past participle to criticize things we or other people did or didn't do in the past — and to say what was the correct or better thing to have done.

🔎 Four Key Sentences
SentenceWhat it shows
1 You really should have contacted me sooner about the problem.you didn't contact me — but it was the right thing to do
2 They shouldn't have spent so much money on a holiday.they spent it — but it was the wrong thing to do
3 Who should we have told about the change of arrangements for the meeting?a question about what was the right thing to do
4 They shouldn't have taken the money.they took it — but it was wrong
Structure
should have + V3

Criticism: the correct or better thing was not done.

Subject + should have + V3
  • I should have waited before I sent the email.
  • She should never have posted the letter.
shouldn't have + V3

Criticism: a wrong thing was done.

Subject + shouldn't have + V3
  • You shouldn't have said anything to him. He's really upset now.
Questions

Asking about the better thing to do.

Question word + should + subject + have + V3?
  • I didn't know what to do. In your opinion, what should I have done?
🗣 In spoken English

We usually contract have to 've.

  • We should've waited a little longer.
  • You shouldn't've said anything.
🔁 Compare: advice now vs criticism of the past
FormUseExample
should + V1advice about the present or futureYou should study more.
should have + V3criticism or regret about the pastYou should have studied more.
⚠ Common Mistakes
Use have + V3
✗ I should have wait.
✓ I should have waited.
✗ She should have went home.
✓ She should have gone home.
have, not of
✗ I should of called you.
✓ I should have called you. / I should've called you.
💡 should of is a spelling mistake that comes from the sound of should've.

take / have (collocations)

take and have are two of the most common verbs in English phrases (collocations). In these phrases the main meaning is not in the verb but in the whole expression — so learn each phrase as one unit.

How to use them

Use the verb that belongs to the phrase. You cannot swap take and have, and you cannot translate the verb word for word.

VOCABULARY 2 OF 4

Appearance

We describe a person's appearance in four main areas: age, build, facial features and hair. Each area uses a slightly different structure.

How to describe people
1. Age
be + adjective | in his / her + twenties
  • He's elderly. / She's middle-aged.
  • She's in her twenties.
💡 elderly is a polite word for old.
2. Build
be + adjective | be in good shape
  • He's slim and well-built.
  • She's in good shape.
  • He's a bit overweight.
💡 overweight is more polite than fat.
3. Facial features
have + a / an + noun | have + plural noun
  • She has a round face and long eyelashes.
  • He has a large jaw and thick eyebrows.
  • He has a double chin.

Facial hair: He has a moustache / stubble. He's clean-shaven.

4. Hair
have + adjective + hair | his / her hair is + adjective
  • She has shoulder-length, curly hair.
  • His hair is spiky / grey / dyed.
  • He has a fringe. / He's going bald.

Prefixes: in- · over- · mis- · re-

A prefix is a group of letters added to the beginning of a word to change its meaning. The spelling of the word itself does not change.

How to use prefixes
PrefixMeaningExamplesIn a sentence
mis-do badly / wronglymisjudge, misbehave, misunderstandChildren sometimes misbehave in public.
re-do againreplace, rearrange, reappearWe had to rearrange the meeting.
in-notinactive, inconvenient, informalThat time is inconvenient for me.
over-too muchoverpriced, overspendThe jacket is overpriced.
1. The word class stays the same
  • misbehave (verb) → behave (verb)
  • inactive (adjective) → active (adjective)
  • overpriced (adjective) → priced (adjective)
2. in- can change its spelling

Before l, m / p and r the prefix changes:

  • il-legal
  • im-possible, im-polite
  • ir-regular
3. Learn prefix + word together
✗ inpossible
✓ impossible
✗ unconvenient
✓ inconvenient
💡 We can't add any prefix to any word — check in a dictionary.
4. More prefixes in your exercises
  • under- = not enough: underpaid · = below: underground, underwater
  • super- = bigger / better than normal: superstore, supermodel, superhuman
