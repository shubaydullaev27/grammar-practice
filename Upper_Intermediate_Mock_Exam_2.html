The Silk Road

A The name Silk Road suggests a single highway across Asia, but it was in fact a network of trade routes that connected China with the Mediterranean for well over a thousand years. The term itself is surprisingly recent: it was introduced by a German geographer in the nineteenth century, long after the routes had declined.

B Few merchants travelled the whole distance. Goods were passed from trader to trader, and cities along the way, such as Samarkand, Bukhara and Kashgar, grew rich as markets and resting places for caravans. Silk was only one of many products. Caravans also carried spices, porcelain, glass, horses, metals and precious stones.

C Daily life along the routes was demanding. Caravans crossed deserts and mountain passes where water was scarce, and travellers faced bandits, storms and disease. To protect them, rulers built roadside inns, known as caravanserais, where merchants could rest, store goods and exchange news. Many of these buildings survive today and give visitors a clear picture of how the trade worked.

D Equally important were the ideas that travelled with the goods. Buddhism spread from India into Central and East Asia along these routes, and later Islam moved in the opposite direction. Technologies such as papermaking and gunpowder were transmitted westwards, while crops such as grapes and cotton reached new regions. Travellers also exchanged languages, music and styles of art, so that cities along the Silk Road developed remarkably mixed cultures.

E The network began to decline when sea routes between Europe and Asia became safer and cheaper, and when the political stability that protected caravans broke down. Many oases that had been prosperous for centuries became quiet towns.

F Individual travellers left vivid accounts of what they saw. Pilgrims, diplomats and merchants wrote about unfamiliar foods, customs and religions, and their descriptions often surprised readers at home. Some of these works were copied again and again, and they influenced the way Europeans and Asians imagined each other for centuries. Modern historians use them carefully, since travellers sometimes repeated stories they had heard without checking whether they were true.

G Today, the Silk Road is attracting attention again. Several countries have joined together to nominate heritage sites for international protection, and tourism and new transport projects are bringing visitors to old caravan cities. For historians, however, its greatest lesson is that cultures have always been shaped by contact with each other.
