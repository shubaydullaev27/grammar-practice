Bees in the city

A Beekeeping is no longer a hobby reserved for the countryside. In recent years, thousands of hives have appeared on the roofs of offices, hotels and apartment buildings in cities such as London, Paris and New York. The trend began as a way of helping bees, whose numbers have fallen in many regions because of disease, pesticides and the loss of wild flowers.

B Surprisingly, cities can be a good home for bees. Farmland often contains vast fields of a single crop, which offers food for only a few weeks, whereas parks, gardens and balconies provide a mixture of flowers that bloom at different times of the year. Urban areas also tend to use fewer pesticides than intensive agriculture, and temperatures are slightly higher, which allows bees to start foraging earlier in spring.

C The idea is not entirely new. Keeping bees in towns was common in earlier centuries, when many households relied on honey as their main sweetener and on wax for candles. The practice declined as sugar became cheap and cities grew more crowded, but the recent revival has been helped by training courses, online communities and clubs where beginners can learn from experienced keepers. Many newcomers say that the hobby has taught them patience as well as biology.

D Urban honey has become popular as well. Because the bees collect nectar from many different trees and plants, each hive produces a flavour that reflects its neighbourhood, and some restaurants now serve honey from their own roofs. Beekeepers say that this connects city residents with the natural world, and many companies use hives as part of their environmental programmes.

E Not everyone is convinced, however. Some ecologists warn that there may now be too many honeybees in certain cities, and that they compete for flowers with wild species such as bumblebees, many of which are more at risk. In their view, planting more flowers and protecting natural habitats would help pollinators more than adding hives.

F Another difficulty is public attitude. Some residents worry about stings, and landlords occasionally refuse permission for hives on their buildings. Experienced keepers answer that honeybees are rarely aggressive away from the hive, and that a well-managed colony poses little danger to neighbours. They add that education matters, because people who understand how bees behave tend to feel less anxious about them.

G The debate has encouraged a new approach. Instead of simply keeping more bees, some cities are creating pollinator corridors, which are connected strips of flowers that allow all kinds of insects to travel safely across the urban landscape.
